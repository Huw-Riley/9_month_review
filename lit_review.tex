\par\noindent The interstellar medium (ISM) is made up of primarily gas, as well as dust and cosmic rays. The ISM can be divided up based on temperature, ionisation and density, it consists of 5 main phases: the cold molecular medium (CMM), which is defined such that the gas is molecular with a temperature range of ${10} - {30}\ K$ and a number density between ${10}^{2} - {10}^{6}\ c{m}^{-3}$; the cold neutral medium (CNM), which is defined such that the gas is mostly atomic with a temperature range of ${30} - {100}\ K$ and a number density between ${1} - {100}\ c{m}^{-3}$; the warm neutral medium (WNM), which is defined such that the gas is mostly atomic with a temperature range of ${6000} - {10000}\ K$ and number densities below ${1}\ c{m}^{-3}$; the warm ionised medium (WIM), which is defined such that the gas is mostly ionised with a temperature range of ${8000} - {10000}\ K$ and number densities around ${0.4}\ c{m}^{-3}$ and the hot ionised medium (HIM), which is defined such that the gas is mostly ionised with a temperature range of ${10}^{6} - {10}^{7}\ K$ and number densities below ${10}^{-2}\ c{m}^{-3}$ \parencite[see][]{ISM}. The focus of this work will be on the CMM.\
\smallskip
\par\noindent Other than primordial elements, every atom formed inside stars. Therefore, initially these elements that make up the gas are hot and have not formed in their molecular state. To get to the temperature of the CMM, the gas must cool. A star ejects matter throughout its life as stellar winds, as well as at the end of its life in different ways depending on its mass. As the gas moves away from its projenitor the number of ionising photons will decrease which will offset the balance between ionisation and recombination.\ At these higher temperatures the cooling from Lyman-$\alpha$ becomes important. The gereral form of the cooling rate of a two level system is shown below. 

\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_{ul} = \dfrac{g_u}{g_l}A_{ul}E_{ul}n_{l}e^{-E_{ul}/k_{B}T}\label{eq:1}
    \tag{1}
\end{equation}

\par\noindent \autoref{eq:1} is the rearranged form of 2.38 in\ \cite{Chemistry}. The formula comes about by assuming LTE and that the number density is much less than the critical density. $g_u$ and $g_l$ are statistical weihts of the energy states, $A_{ul}$ is the Einstein A coefficient of spontaneous emission, $E_{ul}$ is the energy of the transition and $n_{l}$ is the number density of the lower state. 

\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_{H\!I} = 7.3\times10^{-19}\text{exp}\left(\dfrac{-118400}{T}\right)n_e n_H\label{eq:2}
    \tag{2}
\end{equation}

\par\noindent \autoref{eq:2} appears as 2.70 in\ \cite{Chemistry}. Above $10^4K$ the exponential term stops being negligible which causes the cooling rate to rise rapidly. This alongside the recombination to form atomic hydrgoen is what facilitates the transition to the WNM.\ At higher metalicities the cooling is not dominated by Lyman-$\alpha$ but by other fine structure transitions from heavier elements.\ Once cooled, \autoref{eq:2} shows a ceiling for the tempature in the WNM above which the cooling rate can rapidly exceed the different heating rates\ \parencite{Heating_Cooling}. A different fine structure transition domminates the cooling in the WNM and facilitates the transition to the CNM, the CII transition. The energy of this transition is $92K$ \parencite{Cooling_Heating} meaning the exponent in \autoref{eq:1} becomes $e^{-92/T}$. This means that below $100K$ the cooling from CII decreases rapidly. On the other hand, the first rovibrational transition $(J = 1\text{–}0)$ of CO emits a photon with a rest frequency of $115.27GHz$ \parencite{CO_1_0}, which gives an energy of $5.53K$, using $h\nu/k_B$. This allows the cooling rate of CO to dominate in this temperature regime and allow the gas to get to a temperature of around 10K. Even though CO is a more efficient coolant, CII seems to be a more dominant cooling mechanism at low metalicity as well as for diffuse gas with densities less than $10^3 cm^{-3}$ \parencite{CO_cooling}. This is partly because at low metalicity, the CO cannot be shielded by the dust and photodissociates by way of the ISRF.\ It is also important to note that\ \cite{CO_cooling} indicate that if the cloud is shielded from the effects of photoelectric heating then cooling from CII can allow the gas to get down to temperatures of $20K$ without the prescense of CO.\
\smallskip
\par\noindent Once there is gas at the typical temperatures of the CMM, molecular hydrogen can begin to form. Molecular hydrogen should form directly between two atoms of hydrogen but the Einstein A coefficient is too small for the process to allow for a photon to be emitted during their collision \parencite{H2_formation}. This means that molecular hydrogen cannot form efficiently this way, therefore another mechanism is required to get the amount of molecular hydrogen expected. The process involves atomic hydrogen sticking to a dust grain and potentially colliding with another atom of hydrogen allowing for the recombination of molecular hydrogen. The energy produced by this is enough to break the bonds between the dust grain and the molecule of hydrogen, allowing for it to return to the CMM \parencite{H2_formation}. This has been confirmed experimentally using various dust grain substitues made of carbon or silicates.\ \cite{dust_grain_experiment} find that the peak efficiency of the recombination occurs below $20K$ for both types of grain. The formation of CO is very complex \parencite[see][]{CO_formation} and can involve different pathways, the simplest of which is the formation from a carbon atom and a hydroxyl radical. Even though it is thought that the gas being in a molecular state is not nessecary for star formation, the cloud being able to shield itself from the ISRF is paramount to the formation of both stars and molecular gas \parencite{CO_cooling}. They find that a cloud of atomic gas is still capable of forming stars at the same rate as if molecular gas is able to form. This however does not change the fact that molecular hydrogen still traces star formation, it just may not trace all star formation. 
\smallskip
\par\noindent Tracing molecular gas is important because it allows for the understanding of the steps that lead to star formation. CO is regarded as a good tracer of molecular gas for its abundance in molecular clouds and spontaeous low energy transitions, like the one described above. The idea is to trace the more abundant molecular hydrogen, which is not directly possible due to its lack of emission at the typical temperatures of the CMM.\ The lowest possible rotational transition of molecular hydrogen is at an energy of $510K$ \parencite{H2_formation}. CO has been the gold standard when it comes to tracing molecular hydrogen, but tracers like HCN or $N_2H^+$ have been fund to trace the dnese gas muich more effectively than CO.\ $N_2H^+$ is almost always being detected above column densties of $10^{22} cm^{-2}$ \parencite{N2H_plus_density}, meaning it is much better at tracing the clumps where stars are likely to be forming.
\smallskip
\par\noindent Regions of the ISM that contain the CMM are also known as molecular clouds. These clouds are the densest constituents of the ISM, the collapse of which leads to star formation. For a cloud to collapse the gravitational force pulling the gas together must overcome that of the internal pressure of the gas. Hydrostatic equilibrium is the point at which both forces cancel one another out. This allows for the derivation of the mass above which a spherical system would collapse, knwon as the Jeans mass.

\begin{equation}
    M_J = \dfrac{\pi^{3/2}{c_s}^3}{{\rho_o}^{1/2}G^{3/2}}\label{eq:3}
    \tag{3}
\end{equation}

\par\noindent \autoref{eq:3} appears as the rearranged form of 9.23 in\ \cite{SF} using the fact that $M_J \sim \rho_o\lambda^3$. $c_s$ refers to the isothermal sound speed in the medium given by $\sqrt{k_B T / \mu m_p}$. This equation assumes that the system is isolated with no external forces, but the ISM is turbulent therefore an external pressure could be present. In this instance the external pressure would oppose the internal pressure of the gas, making it easier for the gas to collapse. This mass is known as the Bonner-Ebert mass.

\begin{equation}
    M_{BE} = \dfrac{1.18{c_s}^4}{{P_o}^{1/2}G^{3/2}}\label{eq:4}
    \tag{4}
\end{equation}

\par\noindent \autoref{eq:4} appears as 9.16 in\ \cite{SF}. $P_o$ is the external pressure on the cloud. There are several mechanisms that affect star formation that these equations do not account for. Magnetic fields
